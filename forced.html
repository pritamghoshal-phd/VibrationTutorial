4Forced harmonic vibration

Now drive the system with a harmonic force:

$$ m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F_0\cos\omega t $$

The solution is the sum of a homogeneous part (the free, decaying vibration from Section 3, called the transient) and a particular part that oscillates at the forcing frequency $\omega$ (the steady state). Assume $x_p = X\cos(\omega t-\phi)$, substitute, and match coefficients. With the frequency ratio $r=\omega/\omega_n$ and static deflection $\delta_{st}=F_0/k$:

$$ \frac{X}{\delta_{st}} = M(r,\zeta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{2\zeta r}{1-r^2} $$
Deriving the particular solution (undetermined coefficients)

Write the forced equation in standard form, $\ddot x+2\zeta\omega_n\dot x+\omega_n^2x=(F_0/M)\cos\omega t$, and guess a response at the forcing frequency containing both a cosine and a sine: $x_p=A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t$. Substituting and collecting terms:

$$\cos\omega t:\quad(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)A+2\zeta\omega_n\omega\,B=\frac{F_0}{M},\qquad \sin\omega t:\quad-2\zeta\omega_n\omega\,A+(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)B=0.$$

These must hold for all $t$, so both coefficient equations must be satisfied. Solving the pair:

$$A=\frac{F_0}{M}\,\frac{\omega_n^2-\omega^2}{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+(2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2},\qquad B=\frac{F_0}{M}\,\frac{2\zeta\omega_n\omega}{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+(2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}.$$

Writing $A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t=X\cos(\omega t-\phi)$ means $A=X\cos\phi$ and $B=X\sin\phi$, so $X=\sqrt{A^2+B^2}$ and $\tan\phi=B/A$. Dividing top and bottom by $\omega_n^4$ gives exactly the $M(r,\zeta)$ and $\tan\phi$ above. A sine forcing $F_0\sin\omega t$ works the same way and gives $x_p=X\sin(\omega t-\phi)$ with the same $X$ and $\phi$.

$M$ is the magnification factor and $\phi$ is how far the displacement lags the force. Three regimes:

$r\ll 1$, stiffness-controlled. $M\approx 1$, $\phi\approx 0$. The mass follows the force quasi-statically: $x\approx F(t)/k$.

$r\approx 1$, resonance (damping-controlled). $\phi = 90^\circ$ and $M=\frac{1}{2\zeta}$. Only damping limits the amplitude. The true peak sits slightly below, at $r=\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$.

$r\gg 1$, mass-controlled. $M\approx 1/r^2\to 0$, $\phi\to180^\circ$. The mass is too heavy to follow and moves opposite to the force.

The undamped case: what a negative amplitude means

With $\zeta=0$ the sine term drops out ($B=0$) and the steady response is simply $x_p=A\cos\omega t$ with

$$A=\frac{F_0/K}{1-r^2}.$$

Below resonance $A$ is positive: the mass moves in phase with the force. Above resonance $A$ is negative: the mass moves opposite to the force (180° out of phase). At $r=1$ it is unbounded. Because only $|1-r^2|$ sets the size, two different frequencies give the same size of response, one in phase and one out of phase; they satisfy $r_1^2+r_2^2=2$. Damping smooths the sudden sign flip into the gradual phase change from 0° to 180° that you saw above.

The response needs about 1/(ζωₙ) seconds to build up. If the sweep spends much longer than that crossing the resonance band (a slow sweep), the response follows the steady-state curve and peaks at r ≈ 1. If it rushes through (a fast sweep), the peak is lower, arrives late (above r = 1), and the mass then rings at its natural frequency. That is why machines are run up quickly through resonance.

Vibrations in the wild

On its opening day in June 2000, London’s Millennium Bridge began swaying sideways. Its lateral natural frequency was close to the rate at which people step side to side, so walkers unconsciously fell into step with the sway and pushed it at resonance. It closed after two days and reopened in 2002 with added dampers.
