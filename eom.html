1Equations of motion

Almost every vibration problem in this course reduces to one equation. The canonical model is a mass $m$ attached to a spring of stiffness $k$ and a viscous damper with coefficient $c$, driven by an external force $F(t)$. We describe its position with one coordinate $x(t)$, measured from the static equilibrium position. One coordinate means one degree of freedom (1-DOF).

Displace the mass by a positive $x$ and give it a positive velocity $\dot x$. The spring pulls back with $kx$, the damper resists with $c\dot x$, and $F(t)$ pushes. Newton's second law, $\sum F = m\ddot x$, gives

$$ F(t) - kx - c\dot x = m\ddot x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F(t)\,} $$

Dividing by $m$ puts it in standard form, which introduces the two numbers that control everything that follows:

$$ \ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_n \dot x + \omega_n^2 x = \frac{F(t)}{m}, \qquad \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} = \frac{c}{c_{cr}} $$

$\omega_n$ is the natural frequency (rad/s) and $\zeta$ is the damping ratio (dimensionless). For any 1-DOF system, however complicated it looks, your job is to reach this form and read off $m_{eq}$, $c_{eq}$, $k_{eq}$.

The building blocks

Every system in this chapter is assembled from four kinds of parts: inertia (particles and rigid bodies, which resist acceleration), springs (force proportional to how much they are deformed), dashpots (force proportional to how fast they are deformed) and external forcing. The kinetics tools are the ones you already know: $\sum\vec F=m\vec a_G$ and $\sum\vec M_A=I_A\vec\alpha$, with A either the center of mass or a fixed point of the body.

Getting spring and dashpot directions right

As a system vibrates, each spring alternates between stretched and compressed, and each dashpot between lengthening and shortening, so their forces keep flipping direction. A free-body diagram, however, is drawn once and has to be valid at every instant. The trick is to draw it for one convenient instant and let the algebra handle the rest:

  1. Assume every coordinate and every velocity is positive ($x>0$, $\dot x>0$, and so on).
  2. From the geometry, decide whether each spring is stretched or compressed and whether each dashpot is lengthening or shortening in that state.
  3. Draw each force the way it acts in that state: a stretched spring or lengthening dashpot pulls on the body; a compressed spring or shortening dashpot pushes. Label it $k\Delta$ or $c\dot\Delta$, where $\Delta$ is the deformation written in terms of the coordinates (for example $\Delta=x$, or $x-x_B$ for a spring whose other end moves).
  4. Do not redraw for other instants. When $x$ or $\dot x$ becomes negative, the label $kx$ or $c\dot x$ becomes negative, which reverses the force automatically.

Recipe: deriving the equation of motion (five steps)

A system may need several coordinates to describe each body, but every system in this chapter has a single degree of freedom: constraints tie the coordinates together, and the goal is one equation of the form $M\ddot x+C\dot x+Kx=f(t)$.

  1. Choose a coordinate system: name each coordinate, its positive direction, and where it is measured from (static equilibrium is usually best).
  2. Draw the free-body diagrams of every body, with all coordinates and velocities positive, using the spring and dashpot rule above.
  3. Kinetics: apply Newton–Euler to each body: $\sum F = m\ddot x$, $\sum M_O = I_O\ddot\theta$.
  4. Kinematics: relate the coordinates (cables, rolling without slip, links) and linearize for small motion: $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, $\cos\theta\approx 1$.
  5. Solve: eliminate the unknown internal forces to reach one equation, put it in standard form, and read off $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$.

Alternative for conservative systems: the energy method. Write $T+V=\text{const}$ and differentiate: $\frac{d}{dt}(T+V)=0$. With $T=\tfrac12 m_{eq}\dot x^2$ and $V=\tfrac12 k_{eq}x^2$ this gives $m_{eq}\ddot x + k_{eq}x = 0$ directly.

Sign check: M, C and K must agree

In a correctly derived equation $M\ddot x+C\dot x+Kx=f(t)$, the three coefficients all have the same sign (all positive, or all negative if you multiplied through by −1). The reason is the solution itself: $\omega_n=\sqrt{K/M}$ and $\zeta=C/2\sqrt{KM}$.

  • If $K$ and $M$ had opposite signs, $\omega_n$ would be imaginary and the motion would grow exponentially without oscillating.
  • If $C$ and $M$ had opposite signs, $\zeta$ would be negative and the oscillations would grow instead of decay.

A passive system of masses, springs and dashpots can do neither. So a sign mismatch means a mistake, usually a force direction on an FBD or a sign in the kinematics. Go back and find it rather than flipping a sign to make it look right. (You can see what the wrong signs would do by typing a negative stiffness or damping into the simulations.)

Why gravity disappears

For a mass hanging on a vertical spring, gravity stretches the spring by $\delta_{st}$ with $k\delta_{st}=mg$. If $x$ is measured from that equilibrium, the spring force is $k(\delta_{st}+x)$ and the $mg$ and $k\delta_{st}$ terms cancel exactly. Gravity shifts where the mass vibrates, not how. (A pendulum is different: there gravity is the restoring force.)

What if we keep gravity? Measure $y$ from the unstretched spring instead. Then $m\ddot y = mg - ky$. Write $y=\delta_{st}+x$ and use $k\delta_{st}=mg$: $m\ddot x = mg - k\delta_{st} - kx = -kx$. Same equation, same $\omega_n$. Gravity only moves the center of the motion down by $\delta_{st}$. Try changing $g$ below: the mass hangs lower on Jupiter, but it bobs at exactly the same rate.

Combining springs

Springs in parallel share the displacement: $k_{eq}=k_1+k_2$. Springs in series share the force: $\frac{1}{k_{eq}}=\frac{1}{k_1}+\frac{1}{k_2}$. Dampers combine the same way.

Linearization in action: the pendulum

For a simple pendulum, $\sum M_O = I_O\ddot\theta$ gives $mL^2\ddot\theta = -mgL\sin\theta$, i.e. $\ddot\theta + \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta = 0$. This is nonlinear. For small angles, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and we get the linear oscillator $\ddot\theta + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0$ with $\omega_n=\sqrt{g/L}$. How small is "small"? Find out below.

Why does $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ work? Compare the two restoring terms as the angle grows:
