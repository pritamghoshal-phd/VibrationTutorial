3Free damped vibration

Add the damper: $m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx=0$. With $x=e^{st}$, the characteristic equation $s^2+2\zeta\omega_n s+\omega_n^2=0$ has roots

$$ s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1} $$
Where does the characteristic equation come from?

Try a solution of the form $x=Ae^{\lambda t}$. Then $\dot x=\lambda Ae^{\lambda t}$ and $\ddot x=\lambda^2Ae^{\lambda t}$, and substituting into $M\ddot x+C\dot x+Kx=0$ gives

$$\left(M\lambda^2+C\lambda+K\right)Ae^{\lambda t}=0.$$

The exponential is never zero, and $A=0$ is the trivial "nothing moves" solution, so any actual motion requires

$$M\lambda^2+C\lambda+K=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda=\frac{-C\pm\sqrt{C^2-4MK}}{2M}=\left(-\zeta\pm\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\right)\omega_n,$$

with $\omega_n=\sqrt{K/M}$ and $\zeta=C/2\sqrt{KM}$. Dividing by $m$ is what turns $s$ (or $\lambda$) into the form shown above.

The sign under the square root splits the behavior into three cases:

Underdamped, $\zeta<1$. Complex roots. The mass oscillates inside a decaying envelope, at the damped natural frequency $\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$:

$$ x(t)=e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left[x_0\cos\omega_d t + \frac{v_0+\zeta\omega_n x_0}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_d t\right] $$

Critically damped, $\zeta=1$. Repeated root. Returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without oscillating: $x(t)=\left[x_0+(v_0+\omega_n x_0)t\right]e^{-\omega_n t}$.

Overdamped, $\zeta>1$. Two real negative roots. A slow, sluggish creep back: $x(t)=C_1e^{s_1 t}+C_2e^{s_2 t}$.

From complex roots to cosines and sines

With two distinct roots the general solution is $x=A_1e^{\lambda_1t}+A_2e^{\lambda_2t}$. In the underdamped case $\lambda_{1,2}=-\zeta\omega_n\pm i\omega_d$, so

$$x=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\left(A_1e^{i\omega_dt}+A_2e^{-i\omega_dt}\right).$$

Euler's formula $e^{\pm i\omega_dt}=\cos\omega_dt\pm i\sin\omega_dt$ turns the bracket into a combination of cosine and sine:

$$x=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\left(C\cos\omega_dt+S\sin\omega_dt\right),\qquad C=A_1+A_2,\quad S=i(A_1-A_2).$$

For a real motion $A_1$ and $A_2$ are complex conjugates, which makes $C$ and $S$ real. The initial conditions give $C=x_0$ and $S=(v_0+\zeta\omega_nx_0)/\omega_d$. With $\zeta=0$ this reduces to the undamped $x=C\cos\omega_nt+S\sin\omega_nt$, which never decays.

The roots $s_{1,2}$ are the whole story. Their real part sets how fast the motion decays, and their imaginary part sets how fast it oscillates. Watch them move as the damping changes:

Underdamped roots slide around the circle toward the real axis; at ζ = 1 they meet; beyond that they split along the real axis. One root then creeps toward zero, which is why heavy damping is slow.

Measuring damping: the logarithmic decrement. For an underdamped system, the ratio of successive peaks is constant. If $x_1$ and $x_{n+1}$ are peaks $n$ cycles apart,

$$ \delta = \frac1n\ln\frac{x_1}{x_{n+1}} = \frac{2\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \zeta=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}} $$

This is how damping is measured in the lab: pluck the structure and read the peaks. The demo below does exactly that on the simulated response.

Different systems, same motion

Divide the equation of motion by $M$ and it becomes the standard form $\ddot x+2\zeta\omega_n\dot x+\omega_n^2x=f(t)/M$. The free response therefore depends on only two numbers, $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$, plus the initial conditions, not on $M$, $C$ and $K$ individually. Two systems that look nothing alike, with different masses, geometry, springs and dampers, will vibrate identically if their $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$ match. The standard form makes that visible at a glance, and it also tells you immediately whether a system is under-, critically or overdamped.

Vibrations in the wild

Old car-mechanic test: push down hard on one corner of a parked car and let go. A healthy suspension settles after about one rebound. If it keeps bouncing, the shock absorbers (the dampers) are worn and ζ has dropped too low.
